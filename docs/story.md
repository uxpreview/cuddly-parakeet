# Story bible: The Long Way Home

Draft 3. Supersedes all Ahead of You material entirely.

**[LOAD-BEARING]** means overturning it changes the whole game.
**[OPEN]** means a decision is still owed.

---

## The one sentence

The dog was never lost.

A boy chases his dog across the Dalmatian coast for a whole day, always a minute
behind, and learns at his own front gate that the dog had been leading him home
the entire time, by the only route the dog knew.

The player thinks this is a game about finding a dog. It is a game about being
found.

---

## The ending, written first

Full dark. The boy comes up the last stretch of beach road and there is the gate,
and there is the dog, sitting in front of it, tail sweeping the dust, exactly as
if nothing happened. The windows of the house are lit warm. Someone opens the
door.

The dog does not run to the boy. The dog waits for the boy to come to him,
because that is what he has been doing all day.

Then the route map: the whole day as one dotted line across a hand-drawn map of
the coast, and seen all at once, the line was never a chase. It was a route.
Every wander had a reason. It bends around the flooded ford, around the cliff
path, around the road. It is the long way home because the long way was the safe
way, and it is drawn like the dog planned it, because he did.

Cut to the title. The game is named after its last image. **[LOAD-BEARING]**

---

## The day

The game is one day, sunrise to dark, and each chapter is a time of day.
**[LOAD-BEARING]**

Color is the clock. The player always knows roughly where they are in the story
by the light, without a single UI element. See `docs/art-direction.md` for the
palettes.

| Chapter | Place | Time | Light |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 The Canyon | River canyon | Morning | Cool gold, long soft shadows |
| 2 The Old Town | Palace town on the harbor | Hard noon | White stone, black shade, teal water |
| 3 The Woods | Pine woods on the ridge | Golden hour into dusk | Amber to blue |
| 4 The Shore | Beach road home | Night | Indigo, moonlight, then lit windows |

Setting: a fictionalized Dalmatian coast. Karst canyon, a town grown inside
Roman palace walls, stone-pine woods, pebble shore. One real, specific,
coherent geography. Invented place names, real textures. **[LOAD-BEARING]**
Specific beats generic, and an amalgam of "Mediterranean vibes" is the named
failure mode of better-funded games than this one.

## The setup

Under two minutes, playable, no cutscene.

Morning. The boy and the dog are at the swimming spot where the canyon meets the
river, a little way from town, further from home than the boy has ever been on
his own. The player gets thirty seconds of ordinary: walking, the dog trotting
at heel, the whistle introduced as a toy rather than a tool. Whistle, and the dog
barks and bounces. That is the whole tutorial.

Then the dog goes rigid, stares up the canyon at something the player never sees,
and bolts.

**[LOAD-BEARING]** The player never learns what the dog saw, because there was
nothing. The bolt is the dog deciding it is time to go home, and the fiction of
chasing something is how a dog gets a boy to follow. This is never stated. On a
second playthrough the stare is visibly aimed at nothing, and that is the reward
for replaying.

---

## The whistle

One button. The entire interaction model beyond walking.

Whistle, and somewhere ahead the dog answers. A bark, birds startling from where
he is, a gate heard swinging. The answer gives a direction, not a marker. There
is no compass, no waypoint, no objective text, ever. The world answers instead.

The whistle's meaning changes each chapter, which is the mechanic-variation
pattern this project keeps on purpose:

| Chapter | What the whistle does |
|---|---|
| 1 Canyon | Teaches itself. Bark answers clean and close. Trust the sound |
| 2 Old Town | The bark echoes off stone and arrives from wrong directions. Sound misleads, eyes take over: tracking, glimpses, knocked-over things |
| 3 Woods | Dark. Sound is everything again, and this time it is honest. The chapter is navigated substantially by call and answer |
| 4 Shore | The dog is beside you. The whistle does nothing but make him look up at you. Pressing it anyway is its own small moment |

**Tracking is the other half of wayfinding.** Pawprints in dust and sand, a
tipped crate, a shaken fence, market cats scattered, a dropped pastry being
fought over by gulls. The trail is diegetic. Reading the world *is* the
navigation system. **[LOAD-BEARING]**

## The rules of the dog

These make the twist fair and the game kind. All four are **[LOAD-BEARING]**.

1. **He is never in danger, and the game never pretends he is.** No whimper
   offscreen, no near-miss with a car, no dog-in-peril beat of any kind. The
   audience for a dog story braces for the worst from minute one. This game
   never touches that wire. The tension is "I am far from home and it is getting
   dark," never "something happened to him."
2. **He always waits at danger.** At anything hazardous, a drop, deep water, a
   road, he is sitting there when the boy arrives, and only moves on once the
   boy is through safely. Players read this as good luck. It is the twist,
   visible from chapter one.
3. **He looks back.** Constantly. Every glimpse of him includes a look back.
4. **He is always slightly out of reach, never fleeing.** Trotting, not
   running. Waiting at bends. If the player does something clever and closes
   the distance fast, he lets them get close enough to almost touch before
   moving on, and that near-miss is staged, not rubber-banded. It should feel
   like almost, not like the game cheating.

The chase must read as a game the dog is playing, in hindsight. First time
through it reads as a dog being a dog.

## Chapters

Total 40 to 50 minutes with wandering. The per-chapter times below are critical
path; wandering supplies the difference.

### Chapter 1: The Canyon
**~8 minutes. Morning. Worry, then rhythm.**

The bolt, then the chase upriver. Narrow paths, shallows to cross, a fallen pine
over a channel. The problems are traversal: finding the way down a ledge,
following prints across a gravel bar, a plank to walk.

The boy is a boy. He cannot climb cliffs, cannot swim the deep channel, cannot
jump far. The dog's route always respects what the boy can do. Nobody says this.

**Mini-arc:** panic settling into determination.
**Ends:** the canyon opens, and there is the town below, the dog visible far down
the switchbacks, looking back. Near-miss number one, staged wide and beautiful.
**Then:** the first route map. The game has been drawing the day.

### Chapter 2: The Old Town
**~12 minutes. Noon. Wonder, and being small.**

A town grown inside ancient palace walls. Alleys, courtyards, washing lines,
market stalls, the harbor. People, and the game's only humans: none of them
speak, all of them react. A fishmonger points the way the dog went. Kids chasing
the same dog for fun. An old woman who clearly knows this dog and gives him
something from her pocket.

That last one is a planted clue: the dog has been here before. The route is
known. **[OPEN]** how many of these "the town knows this dog" moments to plant.
Recommend three. One is missable.

Sound misleads here, eyes lead. The densest chapter, the most optional space,
the most alive. Problem solving is spatial: a locked gate means over the wall
walk, a blocked alley means through the courtyard, glimpses through fences
steer the route.

**Mini-arc:** worry forgotten, the chase becomes an adventure.
**Ends:** the dog slips out the land gate at the top of town as the light goes
long. Near-miss number two: close enough this time that the boy touches the
collar. It slips through his fingers.

### Chapter 3: The Woods
**~10 minutes. Golden hour into dusk. The dark walk.**

Stone pines on the ridge above the coast. The light dies across this chapter as
the boy moves through it, keyed to route progress rather than clock time (see
`docs/game-design.md`), amber to blue to genuinely dark, and the game's tone
shifts with it. A wandering player is never punished with darkness they did not
choose.
This is the chapter that earns the ending, and the closest the game comes to
fear, all of it environmental: shapes, wind, the boy's own smallness. Never the
dog.

The whistle becomes the whole world. Call, answer, walk toward it, call again.
The bark is always calm. Somewhere in the dark, one pair of eyes catches
moonlight, and it is him, waiting, and the exhale of that moment is the point
of the chapter.

He stays closer here. Rule 2 is doing visible work: at the ravine, at the old
wall, he is sitting there each time, waiting.

**Mini-arc:** fear walked through, not around, with company just ahead.
**Ends:** the treeline breaks onto the shore under a risen moon, and the dog does
not run on. He sits, and waits, and when the boy reaches him he stands and falls
in beside him. The chase is over and nothing needs to say so.

### Chapter 4: The Shore
**~6 minutes. Night. Together.**

The whole chapter is the two of them walking the beach road home, side by side.
No problems to solve, no misdirection, no whistle needed. The moon on the water,
the sound of it, the dog at heel where he was at minute one.

This chapter is short and it must not be rushed in production, because it is the
release the whole game has been holding. Everything the player felt about being
lost resolves into the specific warmth of walking somewhere at night with a dog
who knows the way.

**Ends:** the gate. The lit windows. The ending written above, then the map, then
the title. **[OPEN]** whether the door is opened by a parent silhouette or just
opens. Recommend silhouette, no face, no voice.

## The route map

At the end of each chapter the day so far appears as a dotted line on a
hand-drawn paper map of the coast, in the tradition of a wandering-child dotted
path. It is charming after chapter 1, funny after chapter 2, and quietly moving
after chapter 4, when the full line reveals the route was a plan.

The line is the boy's actual walked path, wander and all. Its spine is still
the dog's route, because the required path is his, and that is why the full
line reads as his plan: the detours around the ford, the cliff and the road are
the shape he chose, and the player's wandering is texture on top of it.

Chapter 3 skips the map. Dusk into night should not be interrupted by paper.
**[LOAD-BEARING]** The map is punctuation, and that beat needs none.

No numbers, no percentages, no completion state, ever.

---

## Exploration

The dog's trail is the required path. Everything off it is optional and the
optional space carries the texture: the town especially. Nothing optional gates
anything. No collectibles, no counter. What wandering earns is more world, more
of the "town knows this dog" clues, and a denser dotted line on the map, which
is its own reward because the map is the proof of how you spent the day.

---

## Narration

**[OPEN]**, and the current recommendation is almost none.

Boy-loses-dog is legible without a single word, and this draft is written so
that it plays wordless. The reveal is carried by the map, the waiting-at-danger
pattern, and the old woman's pocket, not by text.

If playtesting shows the middle sagging, the fallback is sparse remembered
narration, an adult voice recalling the day from years later, roughly 300 words
total, appearing at chapter transitions only, never during play. Sample
register, for judging the voice and nothing else:

> I thought I was the one doing the finding.

> He waited at the ravine. He waited at the wall. I told everyone it was luck.

> The long way home was the only way he knew. It was the way we had always gone.

Decide after the grey-box playtest, not before. Text added to a game that has
not been played yet is text added to a guess.

## Never stated

- What the dog bolted at (nothing)
- That the dog is leading (shown, never told)
- Why the long way was the safe way (the map implies it, nothing confirms it)
- The town's history, the boy's name, the dog's name **[OPEN]**, the dog's name
  might be the one word the game contains, on the menu screen, as the title of
  the save file. Recommend deciding this late

## Tone guardrails

Warm, not cozy. Cozy is a market category and a flattening. This game has real
dusk-fear in chapter 3 and real ache in chapter 4, and it earns its warmth by
walking through both. What it never has: peril to the dog, cruelty, irony,
a villain, a fail state, or a joke at the boy's expense.

---

## Prior art to differentiate from

**A Short Hike** owns warm-exploration-with-a-goal-you-stop-caring-about. Ours
differs by urgency: the player cares about the goal the whole way, and the game
is linear by design, a day with a shape rather than an open playground.

**Old Man's Journey** owns flat-color Mediterranean melancholy. Ours differs by
tense and temperature: a child moving toward something, not an old man moving
back through regret, and one continuous day rather than a life in vignettes.

**The Last Guardian** owns creature-companion trust, built on the creature
disobeying. Ours inverts it: the dog was obeying a plan the whole time, and the
player finds out last.
